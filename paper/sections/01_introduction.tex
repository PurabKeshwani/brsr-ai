\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

India's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) framework provides a common structure for environmental, social, and governance (ESG) disclosures. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) introduced mandatory BRSR reporting for the top 1,000 listed companies by market capitalisation from financial year 2022--23. The framework covers nine principles of responsible business conduct, with mandatory essential indicators and voluntary leadership indicators \cite{sebi2021}. Each annual report combines written explanations with numerical information. In this paper, a \emph{text segment} is a selected narrative response, and a \emph{fact} is an individual reported value stored with the relevant reporting context.

Manual review involves checking the evidence behind a company's written claims. An analyst must identify the relevant BRSR principle and find the numerical data needed to assess each claim. This requires checking what the numbers measure, the units used, the reporting period, and the company operations covered by the data, known as the business boundary. For example, assessing a claim that \emph{water use decreased} requires comparing reported water-use values for the current and previous reporting years. The values must refer to the same measure and company operations, with units made consistent before comparison. Comparisons between companies must also account for differences in business activities and the information each company is expected to report. Repeating these checks across hundreds of reports makes manual analysis time-consuming and difficult to manage.

Existing automated methods support several steps in this review process.
ClimateBERT analyses climate-related language \cite{webersinke2021}.
ChatReport answers questions about reports and links the answers to the relevant report text \cite{ni2023}.
ESGReveal extracts structured information from ESG documents \cite{zou2025}.
These methods show how language models can help analyse disclosures and find relevant report content.
A connected BRSR workflow must also link claims to reported evidence, explain how training labels are assigned, and show how company comparisons and scores are calculated.
Research on greenwashing detection distinguishes language analysis from checking whether a claim is supported by evidence \cite{calamai2025}.
Studies of Indian BRSR disclosures provide context for understanding differences in reporting practices across companies \cite{garg2025}.

GRASP brings these requirements together through a shared report database, a classifier for the nine BRSR principles, review checks linked to reported evidence, and a Transparency Score based on four components. Report tags and concept mappings generate \emph{weak labels}, meaning principle labels derived from the report structure rather than independent manual annotation. The resulting dataset contains 42,409 labelled text segments. Separate groups of companies are used for supervised training, validation, and testing. Evaluation also compares results across label sources and on reporting concepts excluded from training. System results are presented at four levels: a detailed example of one company, comparisons between selected financial companies, the complete financial-sector group, and all recorded sectors. The main contribution is a connected workflow that links analysis outputs to source reports and clearly states the data and the labels or ratings used for each evaluation.

The remaining sections follow the development and evaluation of this workflow. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews related research, and Section~\ref{sec:proposed} presents the proposed solution. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} explains the seven modules and score calculations. Sections~\ref{sec:evaluation} and~\ref{sec:results} report model evaluations and company, sector, and year-wise results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper and outlines future work. Detailed sector and paired-year tables accompany the results.
