\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{Report acquisition and structured storage}
\label{sec:ingestion}

BRSR reports are collected in eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL), a digital format that associates reported values with machine-readable labels and context. A download log records completed and failed files so that collection can resume after an interruption. The parser reads the structured records directly. A \emph{concept} names a reporting item, such as total water withdrawal; the \emph{context} identifies the company, period, and reporting categories; and the \emph{unit} specifies the measurement scale \cite{xbrl2013}. Preserving these fields allows later checks to identify which figures are relevant to a claim.

The database links company, report, and fact records. Company records store the Corporate Identity Number (CIN), name, and industry code. Report records store the company link, reporting dates, and source location. Fact records preserve the concept, original value, unit, stated precision, period, and dimensions. Dimensions describe categories such as gender or employment type, allowing category-specific values to remain separate. Values are retained as text and converted to numbers when arithmetic is required. A company--year uniqueness constraint prevents duplicate report ingestion. The resulting database supplies the source records for text preparation, numerical checks, and score calculation.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{Text segment selection and weak-label construction}
\label{sec:labels}

Candidate text segments are extracted from concepts containing \artifact{ExplanatoryTextBlock}, which store narrative reporting responses. The builder removes URLs and retains a candidate only when at least 15 useful words remain. Bare links, short fragments, and configured non-answers are excluded. Each retained text segment receives a principle label from three sources in priority order: an explicit \artifact{PrincipleAxis} tag in Section B, a Section C concept-to-principle mapping, or a numbered \artifact{NotesPrincipleX} concept. The Section C extension contains 29 AI-assisted assignments without independent expert verification. Existing configured mappings take priority where mapping sources overlap.

Candidates are grouped by company, concept, and cleaned text. Repeated entries carrying the same label become one dataset row, meaning one text segment stored with a principle assignment. Groups carrying several labels are excluded from the single-label dataset; these groups include cross-principle responses and generic text reused under different questions. Every row retains the label source for evaluation, while only the text enters the classifier. The resulting weak labels serve as the training and evaluation reference, so classifier agreement measures consistency with the report-derived assignments.

The final dataset contains 42,409 labelled text segments. Splitting by CIN assigns 29,834 text segments from 814 companies to training, 6,374 from 175 companies to validation, and 6,201 from 175 companies to testing. No company appears in more than one supervised split. Exact text shared by different companies is assessed separately. Company scoring uses a different text-selection rule, described in Section~\ref{sec:score}; the classifier and scoring counts therefore refer to different retained text groups.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{Domain adaptation and principle classification}
\label{sec:classifier}

The classifier is based on \artifact{bert-base-uncased}, a general-English BERT model whose tokenizer treats uppercase and lowercase forms alike \cite{devlin2019}. Development first adapts the encoder, the part of BERT that represents input meaning, through \emph{masked language modelling}. Selected word pieces are hidden in BRSR text and predicted from the surrounding context. This step uses unlabelled text to learn reporting vocabulary before principle classification \cite{gururangan2020}. The BERT architecture is retained throughout; domain adaptation changes the learned parameters rather than replacing the model.

Supervised fine-tuning replaces the word-prediction output with a nine-output classification layer and updates both the encoder and the new layer using the labelled training text segments. The tokenizer converts word pieces, called \emph{tokens}, into numerical identifiers. Inputs are truncated at 512 tokens, and dynamic padding adds only the padding required within each batch. An attention mask distinguishes text positions from padding. Company identifiers, concept names, and label-source fields support grouping and evaluation but are not classifier inputs.

Training runs for five epochs, with one epoch representing a complete pass through the training group. Batches contain four text segments, and gradient accumulation over four batches gives an effective batch size of 16. The learning rate is $2\times10^{-5}$, with 300 warmup steps followed by cosine decay; weight decay is 0.01. Weighted cross-entropy penalises low probability for the recorded label. Class weights use inverse training frequencies, square-root smoothing, and rescaling to a mean of one, increasing the contribution of less frequent principles without applying the full inverse-frequency difference.

Validation macro-F1 selects the checkpoint, or saved parameter set, after each epoch. Five seeds---42, 1, 7, 99, and 2024---were compared on the same training and validation groups. A seed controls random choices such as data order and classification-layer initialisation. Seed 99 supplied the selected \artifact{checkpoint-7460}. LayerNorm keeps internal values on a stable scale. Recovery mapped 50 LayerNorm parameters from the older \artifact{gamma}/\artifact{beta} names to \artifact{weight}/\artifact{bias}, required all parameters to load, and verified every parameter value after saving and reloading. Recovery involved no further training. Section~\ref{sec:training_history} presents the recorded training history and recovered-model results.

For a text segment $x$, the classifier produces nine raw scores, or \emph{logits}. Softmax converts logits $z_1,\dots,z_9$ into probabilities:
\begin{equation}
p_k(x)=\frac{\exp(z_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{9}\exp(z_j)}.
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
Here, $p_k(x)$ is the model probability for principle $k$, and the nine probabilities sum to one. The highest probability gives the Top-1 prediction, while the two highest give the Top-2 candidate list. The recovered BERT checkpoint supplies the final classifier and the service predictions. MPNet supports semantic comparison in other modules; historical ClimateBERT and ensemble experiments are not part of the final classifier.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{Vagueness detection and report-consistency checks}
\label{sec:detector}

This module combines a language branch for vagueness detection with numerical rules for claim--figure, assurance-gap, and omission checks. The language branch uses both wording and evidence markers to identify text segments for review. The same detector supplies Clarity in Section~\ref{sec:score}. Numerical checks produce separate review records containing the relevant disclosure and reported values; these records do not enter the Transparency Score.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Hedging words and evidence markers}

The lexical detector searches text segments for promises, conditional commitments, unclear time expressions, and unmeasured progress claims. Evidence patterns recognise percentages, currency amounts, physical quantities, workforce or incident counts, reporting years, named standards, and selected formal governance systems. A matched pattern sets the \artifact{has_kpi} marker, which records the presence of an evidence indicator rather than verifying the underlying claim. For example, \emph{emissions fell by 12\% in FY2025--26} contains a percentage and reporting year, whereas \emph{the company aims to improve water use in the coming years} contains hedging without a reported measure.

The lexical rule flags hedging without an evidence marker. For text segments longer than 80 words, hedging density must also reach 0.005, where density is the number of matches divided by the word count. A separate rule flags bare URLs and configured website redirects. Currency matches require digits, and the pattern set includes MW/GW units and statutory terminology. The resulting hedging, evidence, and URL indicators support the final language decision; the evidence marker also supplies Specificity in the scoring module.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Comparing sentence meanings}

The \artifact{all-mpnet-base-v2} encoder represents sentences as vectors, and cosine similarity compares each sentence with fixed vague and specific reference statements \cite{reimers2019}\cite{song2020}. For sentence $j$, let $v_j$ be the highest similarity to a vague reference and $q_j$ the highest similarity to a specific reference. The detector selects the sentence with the largest $q_j-v_j$, representing the strongest specific wording within the text segment, and applies the decision thresholds to that sentence.

Without an evidence marker, the semantic rule flags a text segment when $v_j\geq0.42$ and $v_j>q_j$. With a marker, flagging requires $v_j\geq0.58$ and $v_j-q_j\geq0.15$. A URL-redirect match takes priority. The final language flag is raised when either the lexical or semantic rule applies. A saved freeze manifest records the detector version and thresholds, which remain unchanged during the reported evaluations. The output identifies potentially vague text for inspection rather than confirming misleading conduct.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Claim--figure, assurance, and omission checks}

The claim--figure rule covers emissions, water, waste, and non-renewable energy. A sentence must contain both improvement wording and a phrase associated with the measure. An increase in the corresponding filed value creates a review record. Matching the improvement and measure within the same sentence reduces unrelated pairings, such as linking a Scope 2 claim with a Scope 1 figure. The record preserves the sentence, concept, previous and current values, and assurance information. Analysts can then check measure definitions, totals versus intensity values, units, periods, and business boundaries against the original report.

The assurance-gap rule connects a domain-specific fact with assurance information for the same domain. The omission rule identifies a concept reported in the previous period without a matching current-period value. These rules retain the implementation's matching conditions: assurance selection does not fully restrict period, unit, and dimension, and zero-valued entries receive different treatment across rules. The assurance rule treats a text zero as missing, while the omission rule skips some previous zero entries. In contrast, Completeness accepts structured zero values. These differences define how review records are generated and why the linked source values remain available. The evaluation of detector accuracy covers the language branch, as specified in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{Principle mapping and the zero-shot comparator}
\label{sec:mapping}

Principle mapping reuses the recovered classifier to summarise support for the recorded principle assignments in a report. For text segments filed under principle $k$, the service averages the corresponding probabilities from Equation~\eqref{eq:softmax} and retains the lowest-scoring text segment for inspection:
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{x\in X_k}p_k(x),
\label{eq:mapmean}
\end{equation}
Here, $X_k$ is the set of text segments assigned to principle $k$, and $N_k=|X_k|$ is the number of text segments in that set. The company summary averages the available principle means, excluding principles without a text segment. These values describe model support for the recorded assignment rather than a legal compliance percentage.

A zero-shot MPNet method provides the evaluation comparator without fitting to the principle-labelled training set. Each principle has an expanded definition and fixed example statements, called \emph{anchors}. The ranking score is
\begin{equation}
z_k(x)=0.50\,d_k(x)+0.50\left[0.70\max_a s_{ka}(x)+0.30\,\operatorname{mean}_a s_{ka}(x)\right].
\label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
In this equation, $d_k(x)$ measures similarity between text segment $x$ and the definition of principle $k$, while $s_{ka}(x)$ measures similarity to anchor $a$. The definition and anchor terms each receive half the weight. Within the anchor term, the best match receives 70\% and the mean similarity receives 30\%. Section~\ref{sec:eval_mapping} compares both methods on the same test text segments and weak-label reference.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{Transparency Score and peer comparison}
\label{sec:score}

The Transparency Score summarises report coverage and disclosure characteristics through four components on a 0--100 scale:
\begin{equation}
TS_f=0.40\,C_f+0.30\,Sp_f+0.20\,Cl_f+0.10\,G_f.
\label{eq:ts}
\end{equation}
For report $f$, $C_f$ is Completeness, $Sp_f$ is Specificity, $Cl_f$ is Clarity, and $G_f$ is SDG breadth. The implementation fixes the weights at 0.40/0.30/0.20/0.10 and rounds each component and the combined score to two decimal places. Report facts supply coverage, retained text segments supply evidence and vagueness counts, and a fixed principle map supplies SDG breadth. The following definitions specify the numerator and denominator of each component.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Completeness: coverage of reporting items}

Let $U$ be the database-wide set of 1,057 distinct concepts, including structured and narrative items. For report $f$, let $A_f$ contain concepts with at least one accepted response. Completeness is
\begin{equation}
C_f=100\,\frac{|A_f|}{|U|}.
\label{eq:complete}
\end{equation}
A concept counts once regardless of the number of dimensional values. The same denominator of 1,057 applies to every report, including concepts used by other sectors; the measure therefore represents coverage of a fixed database checklist. Blank values and placeholders such as \emph{NA} and \emph{not applicable} are rejected, while structured zero is accepted. Narrative text blocks reject placeholders and lone zero values. Text longer than 30 characters must contain at least ten words; shorter values are accepted when no placeholder matches. Address fields follow a separate acceptance rule.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Specificity: presence of evidence markers}

Scoring retains text-block facts containing at least 30 characters and removes duplicate concept--text pairs after trimming extra spaces. Identical wording under different concepts may therefore count separately. Let $N_f$ be the retained text-segment count and $K_f$ the count containing at least one evidence marker from Section~\ref{sec:detector}. Specificity is
\begin{equation}
Sp_f=100\,\frac{K_f}{N_f}.
\label{eq:specificity}
\end{equation}
The numerator counts text segments with a recognised marker, not individual numbers or standards. A text segment containing several measurements contributes once. This selection rule differs from the classifier's minimum of 15 useful words, so scoring and classification use different text groups.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Clarity: absence of a vagueness flag}

Clarity reuses the detector from Section~\ref{sec:detector}, with the same word lists, semantic thresholds, and URL rules. Let $V_f$ be the number of flagged scoring text segments. Using the same denominator $N_f$ as Specificity,
\begin{equation}
Cl_f=100\left(1-\frac{V_f}{N_f}\right).
\label{eq:clarity}
\end{equation}
A high Clarity value indicates that few retained text segments were flagged as vague. Concrete evidence is measured separately by Specificity, so a report can have high Clarity and low Specificity. The implementation returns zero for both components when no scoring text segments are available. Clarity contributes 20\% of the combined score; claim--figure, assurance-gap, and omission records remain separate review outputs.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{SDG breadth: coverage under a fixed principle map}

SDG breadth measures how many of the 17 United Nations Sustainable Development Goals are reached through a fixed mapping from the nine BRSR principles \cite{un2015}. A principle qualifies when an explicitly tagged narrative has at least 20 characters and contains an evidence marker. Let $D_f$ contain the qualifying principles, and let $M(k)$ denote the goal numbers assigned to principle $k$. The fixed mapping is
\[
\begin{aligned}
M(1)&=\{16\}, & M(2)=M(9)&=\{9,12\},\\
M(3)&=\{3,5,8\}, & M(4)&=\{10,17\},\\
M(5)&=\{8,10,16\}, & M(6)&=\{6,7,12,13,15\},\\
M(7)&=\{16,17\}, & M(8)&=\{1,8,10,11\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The percentage of distinct mapped goals is
\begin{equation}
G_f=100\,\frac{\left|\bigcup_{k\in D_f}M(k)\right|}{17}.
\label{eq:sdg}
\end{equation}
The union counts each goal once, including a goal reached through several principles. The fixed map covers 14 goals, giving a maximum of 82.35\% against the denominator of 17. This component measures mapped breadth rather than achievement of individual SDG targets. Saved exports use the name \emph{SDG Alignment}; the paper uses \emph{SDG breadth} to describe the calculation. If a duplicate concept--text pair appears under several principles, database record order determines the retained principle.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Sector grouping and percentiles}

Companies are assigned to recorded sectors using industry codes and a local mapping. The analysis retains Financial Services (excl. Insurance) as a separate group from insurance and auxiliary financial services. For company $c$ in sector $S$, the percentile is
\begin{equation}
P_c=100\,\frac{\#\{j\in S:TS_j<TS_c\}}{|S|}.
\label{eq:percentile}
\end{equation}
The numerator counts sector companies with a strictly lower Transparency Score, and $|S|$ is the total sector size. Equal scores receive equal percentiles, and the highest percentile need not reach 100. The main sector comparison includes groups with at least five companies and reports the mean, median, range, and standard deviation of company scores. The service returns the combined score, component percentages, and sector percentile so that a company comparison can be traced to the underlying calculation.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{Service layer and runtime traceability}
\label{sec:api}

An API exposes company search, report analysis, score breakdowns, peer comparisons, and principle mapping using the stored records and analysis outputs. The principle-mapping response identifies the method as \artifact{recovered_seed99_supervised_classifier} and returns the recovered checkpoint path. An unavailable classifier produces an error rather than a silently substituted method. Model-loading status, principle probabilities, review records, and peer comparisons occupy separate response fields. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports the request used to verify this connection; service verification is distinct from model-performance evaluation.
