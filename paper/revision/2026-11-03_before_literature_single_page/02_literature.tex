\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}

Research on automated report analysis examines how methods can help readers understand sustainability disclosures. Existing methods identify the topics discussed, find company commitments, and locate information relevant to a claim. These tasks serve different purposes: finding a promise to reduce emissions, for example, does not confirm that emissions have decreased. Calamai et al. explain this distinction and discuss difficulties in obtaining suitable datasets and evaluating greenwashing detection methods \cite{calamai2025}. The following review begins with language models and methods for creating training labels, followed by research on climate topics, company commitments, and disclosure quality. The final part examines tools for analysing complete reports and studies of Indian sustainability disclosures.

\subsection{Language models and training labels}

BERT first learns how words are used in context by training on large text collections, this process is called pretraining. The model can then be trained further on labelled text to assign categories. This step is called fine-tuning for classification \cite{devlin2019}. Gururangan et al. show that further pretraining on text from a particular domain and task improves several classification tasks \cite{gururangan2020}. This process, called domain adaptation, helps a general language model learn specialised vocabulary before training for the final classification task. Sentence-BERT supports sentence comparison by converting sentences into numerical vectors, or lists of numbers that represent sentence meaning \cite{reimers2019}. Cosine similarity compares these vectors to find sentences with related meanings. This comparison helps find related statements in a report, but similar meaning alone does not confirm that a claim is supported by facts.

Training a classifier normally requires text with known labels. Weak supervision reduces manual labelling by assigning labels through rules or other available information. Snorkel combines the outputs of labelling rules and estimates uncertainty in the assigned labels \cite{ratner2017}. Recording label provenance, meaning where each label came from, is important because one incorrect rule can affect many training records. Kapoor and Narayanan explain how data leakage and unsuitable evaluation methods can make reported results appear better than justified \cite{kapoor2023}. Leakage can occur when test data influence model development, so the test no longer measures performance on separate data. These studies support recording label sources, keeping evaluation groups separate, checking for repeated text, and confirming that the correct saved model produces the reported results.

\subsection{Climate topics, commitments, and disclosure quality}

ClimaText provides sentences labelled according to whether climate change is discussed, supporting climate-topic detection \cite{varini2020}. ClimateBERT uses further pretraining on climate text and includes tasks for evaluating climate-related language \cite{webersinke2021}. Bingler et al. apply ClimateBERT to disclosures under the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) framework and identify vague language and selective reporting \cite{bingler2022}. Selective reporting means emphasising some information while leaving out other relevant details. A later study examines links between \emph{cheap talk}, meaning climate statements that provide few concrete details, and company outcomes such as emissions growth and risks to a company's reputation \cite{bingler2024}. These findings show links between disclosure language and company performance. Such statistical relationships across companies do not establish whether an individual claim is supported by evidence.

ClimateBERT-NetZero uses approximately 3,500 text segments labelled by experts to classify net-zero and reduction commitments and answer questions about the targets \cite{schimanski2023}. The study treats finding a commitment and extracting the target details as separate tasks. Vinella et al. study greenwashing risk classification using generated labels and a fine-tuned ClimateBERT model \cite{vinella2024}. Generated labels can provide more training data, but the results must be interpreted in relation to how the labels were created. Across these studies, identifying a climate topic, finding a commitment, assessing the wording, and measuring progress remain separate tasks.

\subsection{Report-level tools and Indian disclosure studies}

Report-level tools help analysts examine complete documents by finding relevant content and linking model responses to the source text. ChatReport combines this search process, called retrieval, with language-model responses and expert feedback to support report analysis \cite{ni2023}. ESGReveal prepares documents, applies standard questions, retrieves relevant content, and uses model agents to extract structured information from ESG reports \cite{zou2025}. Model agents are software components that use a language model to carry out specific tasks. In both systems, a reviewer can follow a source link to read the supporting statement and surrounding text. This connection helps explain how a model response relates to the original report.

Research on Indian disclosures includes automated text classification and studies of reporting practices. Shankar and Xu describe sustainability text labelled by experts and a supervised BERT classifier for detecting greenwashing at the sentence level \cite{shankar2025}. The study appears as a working paper on the Social Science Research Network (SSRN), posted in 2025, while the manuscript is dated 2024. Garg et al. examine BRSR disclosures from 1,012 businesses for 2022--23 and describe reporting practices \cite{garg2025}. This institutional study describes reporting patterns and does not evaluate a machine-learning model. \subsection{Research findings and motivation}

The reviewed studies support combining language analysis with evidence from the original reports, clearly recorded label sources, and company comparisons that are easy to interpret. GRASP brings these requirements together for structured Indian BRSR reports through a shared database, a classifier for the nine BRSR principles, review checks, and a Transparency Score based on four components. The database keeps reported values with the context needed to interpret the values. Label records explain how each text segment receives a principle label, while the score breakdown shows how individual components contribute to differences between company scores. Evaluation compares classifier results across label sources, separate company groups, and reporting concepts excluded from training. Each comparison examines a different aspect of classifier behaviour. These functions connect report information, model outputs, and company scores within one BRSR review workflow.
