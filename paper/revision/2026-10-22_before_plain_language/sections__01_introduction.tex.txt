\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

India's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting framework places environmental, social, and governance information within a common reporting structure. The 2021 circular issued by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) introduced mandatory BRSR reporting for the top 1,000 listed companies by market capitalisation from financial year 2022--23. The framework follows nine principles of responsible business conduct and distinguishes mandatory essential indicators from voluntary leadership indicators \cite{sebi2021}. A common form improves organisation, but a completed form does not establish the quality or correctness of every response.

A data analyst reviewing a filing must perform several manual tasks. A narrative paragraph must first be located and assigned to a topic. A relevant numerical item must then be found, followed by the correct reporting year, unit, and population. A comparison across companies adds another difficulty because business activities and reporting applicability differ. Repeating such checks across hundreds of filings consumes substantial time. A statement such as ``water use decreased'' illustrates the problem: the direction of change cannot be assessed safely until the reported water measure, reporting boundary, and comparison period have been identified.

Automated text analysis can help with this workload. ClimateBERT identifies climate-related language, specificity, and related categories; ChatReport retrieves passages and produces traceable responses to reporting questions; ESGReveal extracts structured information from narrative reports \cite{webersinke2021,ni2023,zou2025}. Such systems show that language models can organise large disclosure collections. However, three practical questions remain for an implementation built around Indian structured filings.

The first question concerns \emph{evidence linkage}. A phrase detector can identify an aspiration, and an embedding model can identify an environmental topic. Neither output alone establishes whether a numerical fact with the same meaning and reporting context supports a claim. A second question concerns \emph{label reliability}. Labels obtained from filing tags can reduce annotation cost, but a model may learn repeated question wording rather than the underlying principle. A third question concerns \emph{score interpretation}. A single company score becomes easier to use only when every component, denominator, sector assignment, and validation source is visible. Existing work already addresses parts of evidence retrieval and disclosure assessment; the contribution here concerns the implementation and evaluation of a connected BRSR workflow rather than a claim that such tasks have never been studied \cite{calamai2025,garg2025}.

GRASP addresses the evidence-linkage question by retaining narrative text and structured facts within one filing-level data model. Language checks generate review signals, while numerical and assurance checks retain the relevant concept and sentence for inspection. GRASP addresses the label-reliability question by documenting three label sources and evaluating performance by source, company partition, and held-out concept. GRASP addresses score interpretation by exposing four explicit components and comparing saved scores within recorded sector groups. The novelty is the combination of filing-level traceability, regulatory weak labels, source-aware evaluation, and a decomposed scoring service in the Indian BRSR setting. None of the resulting outputs establishes deceptive intent or legal compliance.

The main contributions are a reproducible 42,409-sample weak-label dataset with a company-separated supervised split; an audited principle classifier with source-specific and concept-disjoint diagnostics; an explicitly scoped text-vagueness evaluation alongside unvalidated filing-consistency checks; and a transparent score presentation that expands from a worked company example to a complete financial-sector view and an all-sector snapshot. Reporting the limits of each result forms part of the contribution: model agreement, human-rating association, descriptive scores, and runtime integration answer different questions.

The remaining sections of this paper follow the path from prior research to operational interpretation. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews language models, greenwashing signals, weak supervision, and Indian disclosure research. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} explains the seven analytical modules and the score calculations. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} defines the evaluation populations and reports the audited model results. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the four reporting levels, beginning with one company and expanding to all sector groups. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} examines validity limits and practical use. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with the supported findings and remaining research needs.
